**Q1.** You are taking the AIGP exam and encounter a scenario question about an AI system that processes employee performance data to make promotion recommendations. The question asks which EU AI Act risk classification applies. You have spent 2 minutes on this question and are stuck between 'high-risk' and 'prohibited'. What is the BEST test-taking strategy?

A) Spend another 2 minutes carefully re-reading the EU AI Act risk classification criteria to make the correct choice
B) Flag the question, move on immediately, and return to it after completing easier questions
C) Choose 'high-risk' because employment decisions are always high-risk under the EU AI Act
D) Eliminate both answers and select a different option since you're uncertain between these two

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct: B**

Flag-and-return strategy is critical for the AIGP exam. With 100 questions in 150 minutes (90 seconds average per question), spending 4+ minutes on one question jeopardizes time for easier questions. Flag and return after banking points on questions you know.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- Spending 4+ total minutes on a single question violates time management principles. You risk running out of time on easier questions worth the same points.
- This is making a guess based on incomplete knowledge rather than using test strategy. Employment AI systems CAN be high-risk, but the question may contain details that make it prohibited (e.g., social scoring). Strategy first, content second.
- This assumes both original answers are wrong, but the scenario likely points to one of them. Eliminating plausible answers without cause is poor test strategy.

**Q2.** A scenario question states: 'An AI system uses biometric categorization to infer emotions of job applicants during video interviews to screen candidates.' Under the EU AI Act, the question asks for the risk classification. Three answer choices are: (A) Prohibited AI practice, (B) High-risk AI system, (C) Limited risk AI system. What is the BEST approach to eliminate wrong answers?

A) Eliminate (C) first because any AI system in employment contexts is at least high-risk under the EU AI Act
B) Eliminate (A) first because biometric categorization is high-risk, not prohibited, under EU AI Act Article 5
C) Eliminate (B) first because the EU AI Act explicitly prohibits emotion recognition in workplace and education contexts per Article 5(1)(f)
D) Read all options carefully and choose based on which sounds most protective of individual rights

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct: C**

EU AI Act Article 5(1)(f) explicitly PROHIBITS biometric categorization systems that infer emotions in workplace and educational institutions (with narrow exceptions). The key constraint here is 'emotion inference' + 'workplace context'. Eliminate (B) high-risk first because this is explicitly prohibited, making the correct answer (A).

**Why the others are wrong:**
- While employment AI systems are generally high-risk (Annex III), this overlooks that emotion recognition in workplace is a PROHIBITED practice under Article 5(1)(f). You must check for prohibited practices before defaulting to high-risk.
- This is incorrect. Biometric categorization CAN be prohibited depending on context. Article 5(1)(f) specifically prohibits emotion recognition in workplace/education. This wrong answer relies on incomplete knowledge of Article 5 prohibitions.
- This is not a strategic approach to elimination. The exam tests specific knowledge of EU AI Act articles, not general principles. You must identify the specific constraint (emotion recognition + workplace) and apply Article 5(1)(f).

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Why the others are wrong:**
- While Domain 5 is heavily weighted, spending 2.5 min/question on ALL Domain 5 questions means 67.5 minutes on just 27 questions, leaving only 82.5 minutes for 73 questions (68 seconds each). This creates time pressure on other domains.
- Rushing and guessing reduces accuracy. Better strategy: flag hard Domain 5 questions, complete easier domains, return with remaining time. You're not significantly behind pace yet.
- The exam is 150 minutes with no scheduled breaks. Taking a 2-minute break puts you behind pace (now 67 minutes for 40 questions = 1.675 min/question). Use flag-and-return instead of breaks.

**Q4.** A scenario question presents an AI system developed by Company A (provider), deployed by Company B (deployer), with data processing by Company C (data processor under GDPR). The question asks who is responsible for conducting a Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA) under GDPR. What is the common trap in this question?

A) Confusing the EU AI Act deployer obligations with GDPR controller obligations—the answer depends on who is the GDPR data controller, not who deploys the AI system
B) Assuming Company A (provider) must do the DPIA because they developed the AI system
C) Assuming all three companies must jointly conduct the DPIA because they all process personal data
D) Confusing DPIA (GDPR Article 35) with fundamental rights impact assessment (EU AI Act Article 27)

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct: A**

Common trap: confusing provider/deployer roles (EU AI Act) with controller/processor roles (GDPR). DPIA is required under GDPR Article 35 by the DATA CONTROLLER, not the AI system deployer. Company B may be the deployer BUT Company C is described as processor, suggesting Company B is the controller. Must identify who determines purposes/means of processing.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- Company A (provider) develops the AI but may not be the data controller. GDPR DPIA obligation falls on the controller (who determines purposes/means), not the technology provider. This confuses provider role with controller role.
- This misunderstands GDPR roles. DPIA is not a joint obligation; it's the controller's obligation (though processor can assist). The question tests whether you know controller = DPIA obligation, not 'everyone involved'.
- This is a different assessment. GDPR DPIA (Article 35) is required for high-risk processing. EU AI Act fundamental rights impact assessment (Article 27) is required for high-risk AI deployers. The question asks about DPIA specifically, testing if you confuse the two frameworks.

**Q6.** A scenario question asks: 'An AI system for credit scoring is subject to GDPR Article 22 restrictions. Which of the following measures allows the system to comply?' You see options mentioning: (A) meaningful human oversight, (B) transparency about the logic involved, (C) right to explanation of specific decisions, (D) opt-out mechanism. What is the key to identifying the correct answer?

A) GDPR Article 22 requires human intervention in the decision (not just oversight), so look for the option that mentions human review/intervention, not just 'oversight'
B) GDPR Article 22 requires transparency, so option (B) is always correct for Article 22 compliance
C) GDPR Article 22 grants a right to explanation, so option (C) is the correct answer
D) GDPR Article 22 is about automated decision-making in general, so any answer mentioning human involvement is correct

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct: A**

GDPR Article 22 prohibits solely automated decision-making with legal/similarly significant effects UNLESS exceptions apply (Article 22(2)). Key exception: explicit consent OR necessary for contract OR authorized by law AND 'suitable measures to safeguard rights' including right to human intervention. 'Meaningful human oversight' (option A) is vague; correct answer must specify human INTERVENTION in the decision, not passive oversight.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- Transparency (Article 13-14 information requirements) is required but is not the Article 22-specific safeguard. Article 22(3) specifically requires 'right to obtain human intervention', not just transparency. This confuses general GDPR transparency with Article 22 safeguards.
- GDPR does not grant a general 'right to explanation' of automated decisions. Article 22(3) grants right to 'obtain human intervention, express point of view, and contest the decision'—not explanation of logic. This is a common misconception; exam tests this distinction.
- Article 22 is NOT about all automated decision-making. It applies to SOLELY automated decisions with LEGAL or SIMILARLY SIGNIFICANT effects. The question specifies Article 22 applies (credit scoring qualifies), so look for Article 22(3) safeguards: human intervention, not just any human involvement.

**Q8.** A scenario question describes an AI system and asks which NIST AI Risk Management Framework function applies to the described activity. The scenario states: 'The organization monitors the deployed AI system for performance degradation and bias drift, comparing outputs against baseline metrics.' What is the key to identifying the correct NIST function?

A) The scenario describes ongoing monitoring post-deployment, which maps to the GOVERN function because governance includes oversight
B) The scenario describes performance and bias monitoring, which maps to the MEASURE function because it involves metrics and measurement
C) The scenario describes monitoring a deployed system, which maps to the MANAGE function because it involves managing operational AI systems
D) The scenario describes comparing against baselines, which maps to the MAP function because mapping establishes context and baselines

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct: C**

NIST AI RMF has four functions: GOVERN (culture, policies, oversight), MAP (context, categorization, risk mapping), MEASURE (metrics, testing, evaluation), MANAGE (ongoing monitoring, response, improvement). 'Monitors deployed system' + 'performance degradation' + 'bias drift' = MANAGE function (ongoing operational monitoring and response). Common trap: confusing MEASURE (pre-deployment testing) with MANAGE (post-deployment monitoring).

**Why the others are wrong:**
- GOVERN function establishes policies, culture, and accountability structures. While governance includes oversight, the scenario describes operational monitoring activity (MANAGE), not governance structure (GOVERN). Exam tests distinguishing governance (policies) from management (operations).
- MEASURE function involves defining metrics, testing, and evaluating AI systems (typically pre-deployment or during validation). The scenario describes ONGOING monitoring of a DEPLOYED system, which is MANAGE. Common trap: 'metrics' appears in scenario, but context (deployed system, ongoing monitoring) indicates MANAGE, not MEASURE.
- MAP function establishes context, categorizes AI systems, and maps risks. Baselines are established during MAP/MEASURE, but COMPARING deployed system outputs against baselines is ongoing operational monitoring = MANAGE function. MAP is context-setting; MANAGE is operational monitoring.

> ⚠️ Frequently missed on the exam — read the options carefully.

</details>
